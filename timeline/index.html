1950s–1960s prehistory

Adjoint methods in optimal control

Key people: Pontryagin, Bellman, Kelley, Bryson, Ho, others

Why it mattersThe mathematical ancestor of backprop: propagate sensitivities backward through time or through a dynamical system. This is the “control theory” root of deep learning.

↑ back to top

1970

Reverse-mode automatic differentiation

Key people: Seppo Linnainmaa

Why it mattersModern backprop is essentially reverse-mode differentiation applied to layered computations. Schmidhuber’s historical account credits Linnainmaa’s 1970 work as the first publication of modern reverse-mode AD.

↑ back to top

1974–1982

Backprop ideas applied toward neural networks/control

Key people: Paul Werbos

Why it mattersWerbos helped connect dynamic programming, control, and neural-network learning. This is a useful bridge from adjoint-state methods to neural nets.

↑ back to top

1982

Hopfield networks

Key people: John Hopfield

Why it mattersNeural nets become energy-based associative memories. This connects neural computation to statistical physics and optimization. Hopfield and Hinton later received the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for foundational work enabling machine learning with artificial neural networks.

↑ back to top

1983–1985

Boltzmann machines

Key people: Hinton, Sejnowski, Ackley

Why it mattersHinton’s early line: stochastic neural networks, energy models, hidden representations, and learning by statistical physics. Nobel materials highlight Hinton’s Boltzmann machine as a key foundation.

↑ back to top

1986

Backpropagation becomes the neural-net learning algorithm

Key people: Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams

Why it mattersThe famous Nature paper popularized backprop for multilayer networks: compute errors, propagate them backward, adjust internal weights. This is the practical birth of modern neural-network training.

↑ back to top

Late 1980s–1990s

Convolutional neural networks for vision

Key people: Yann LeCun, Bottou, Bengio, Haffner, others

Why it mattersBackprop meets weight sharing and locality. CNNs become the architecture for images, handwriting, and eventually computer vision. LeCun’s 1989 work on backprop for handwritten zip-code recognition is an early practical anchor.

↑ back to top

1991

Vanishing-gradient problem identified

Key people: Sepp Hochreiter, later Bengio et al.

Why it mattersDeep and recurrent nets are theoretically trainable, but gradients vanish or explode over long chains. This explains why “depth” and “memory” remained hard. Bengio’s 1994 work is a classic treatment of why learning long-term dependencies with gradient descent is difficult.

↑ back to top

1991

Neural history compressor

Key people: Schmidhuber

Why it mattersEarly self-supervised / hierarchical sequence-learning idea: compress history, learn at multiple timescales, use unsupervised pretraining-like stages. This is a strong “pre-foundation-model” anchor.

↑ back to top

1997

LSTM

Key people: Hochreiter, Schmidhuber

Why it mattersLong Short-Term Memory directly attacks the vanishing-gradient problem in recurrent networks. It becomes the dominant architecture for speech, handwriting, translation, and sequence modeling before transformers.

↑ back to top

2000–2003

Neural probabilistic language models

Key people: Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent, Janvin

Why it mattersA major precursor to embeddings and neural language modeling: learn distributed word representations jointly with a probability model over sequences. This is one of the roots of today’s language models.

↑ back to top

2002

Contrastive divergence and RBMs

Key people: Hinton

Why it mattersHinton introduces a practical way to train energy-based models such as restricted Boltzmann machines, setting up the 2006 deep-belief-net revival.

↑ back to top

2006

Deep belief nets and greedy layer-wise pretraining

Key people: Hinton, Osindero, Teh; Bengio, Lamblin, Popovici, Larochelle

Why it mattersThis is the “deep learning revival.” The key idea: train deep models layer by layer, often unsupervised, then fine-tune. It made depth feel trainable again before the GPU/ImageNet era.

↑ back to top

2009

“Learning Deep Architectures for AI”

Key people: Bengio

Why it mattersBengio helps consolidate deep learning as a research program: representation learning, hierarchy, distributed representations, unsupervised pretraining, and why depth matters.

↑ back to top

2011

GPU CNNs begin winning vision contests

Key people: Cireșan, Meier, Schmidhuber, IDSIA

Why it mattersBefore AlexNet, GPU-trained CNNs were already showing that compute plus convnets could dominate visual recognition tasks. This is a useful reminder that the 2012 breakthrough had several converging precursors.

↑ back to top

2012

AlexNet wins ImageNet

Key people: Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton

Why it mattersThe public “deep learning moment.” A large GPU-trained CNN dramatically beats the field on ImageNet. This changes computer vision, then all of ML.

↑ back to top

2012

Dropout

Key people: Hinton, Srivastava, Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Salakhutdinov

Why it mattersDropout becomes a simple, widely used regularization method. The original paper emphasizes randomly omitting feature detectors during training to reduce overfitting.

↑ back to top

2013

Word2vec and modern embeddings

Key people: Mikolov et al.

Why it mattersDistributed word vectors become practical at web scale. This helps shift NLP from sparse symbolic features to dense learned representations.

↑ back to top

2014

Sequence-to-sequence learning

Key people: Sutskever, Vinyals, Le

Why it mattersEncoder-decoder neural networks show that a neural model can map one variable-length sequence to another. This becomes central for translation, summarization, and language tasks.

↑ back to top

2014–2015

Neural attention for translation

Key people: Bahdanau, Cho, Bengio

Why it mattersAttention lets a model dynamically focus on relevant parts of the input sequence instead of compressing everything into one vector. This is a direct ancestor of transformers.

↑ back to top

2014

GANs

Key people: Goodfellow, Pouget-Abadie, Mirza, Xu, Warde-Farley, Ozair, Courville, Bengio

Why it mattersGenerative modeling becomes adversarial: one network generates, another discriminates. This reshapes image generation and representation learning.

↑ back to top

2015

Batch normalization

Key people: Ioffe, Szegedy

Why it mattersStabilizes and accelerates training of deep nets, allowing higher learning rates and reducing sensitivity to initialization.

↑ back to top

2015

ResNet

Key people: He, Zhang, Ren, Sun

Why it mattersResidual connections make extremely deep networks trainable. The idea is simple but profound: learn residual updates around identity paths.

↑ back to top

2015

Deep learning becomes the dominant paradigm

Key people: LeCun, Bengio, Hinton

Why it mattersThe Nature review by LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton defines deep learning as multilayer representation learning trained by backprop and summarizes its breakthroughs in speech, vision, object recognition, drug discovery, and genomics.

↑ back to top

2016

AlphaGo

Key people: DeepMind

Why it mattersDeep neural networks combine with reinforcement learning, self-play, and search. This is important for your “after deep learning” framing: the breakthrough was not just a neural net, but a neural net embedded in a search/planning system.

↑ back to top

2017

Transformer: “Attention Is All You Need”

Key people: Vaswani et al.

Why it mattersSelf-attention replaces recurrence and convolution for many sequence tasks. Transformers are more parallelizable and become the backbone of modern language, vision-language, and multimodal foundation models.

↑ back to top

2018

BERT and self-supervised pretraining

Key people: Devlin, Chang, Lee, Toutanova

Why it mattersBidirectional transformer pretraining turns NLP into “pretrain once, fine-tune broadly.” This is a major step toward foundation models.

↑ back to top

2018

Neural ODEs reconnect deep learning to adjoints

Key people: Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt, Duvenaud

Why it mattersContinuous-depth models treat neural networks as differential equations and use adjoint methods for memory-efficient training. This is a beautiful loop back to your 1970s starting point.

↑ back to top

2020

Scaling laws

Key people: Kaplan et al.; OpenAI

Why it mattersModel loss follows predictable power laws with compute, data, and parameter count over many orders of magnitude. This changes the field’s strategy from “invent the clever architecture” to “scale the right recipe.”

↑ back to top

2020

GPT-3

Key people: Brown et al.; OpenAI

Why it matters175B-parameter language model demonstrates few-shot and in-context learning at a scale that makes language models feel like general-purpose systems.

↑ back to top

2020

Diffusion models mature

Key people: Ho, Jain, Abbeel; Song et al.; others

Why it mattersDenoising diffusion becomes a dominant generative paradigm, eventually powering high-quality image, audio, video, and protein/structure generation. DDPM is a key anchor.

↑ back to top

2021

CLIP and text-image representation learning

Key people: OpenAI

Why it mattersNatural-language supervision trains visual models that transfer zero-shot to many tasks. This is a key bridge from supervised vision to multimodal foundation models.

↑ back to top

2021

AlphaFold2

Key people: DeepMind

Why it mattersDeep learning solves a long-standing protein-structure prediction problem at near-experimental accuracy for many proteins. The 2024 Nobel Chemistry announcement credits AlphaFold2 with predicting the structure of almost all known proteins.

↑ back to top

2022

InstructGPT / RLHF

Key people: OpenAI; Christiano-style RLHF lineage

Why it mattersLanguage models become more useful by training on human preferences and instructions. This is the shift from “predict internet text” to “behave as an assistant.”

↑ back to top

Nov. 2022

ChatGPT

Key people: OpenAI

Why it mattersConversational LLMs become mainstream. OpenAI introduced ChatGPT as a model that interacts conversationally, handles follow-ups, admits mistakes, and challenges incorrect premises.

↑ back to top

2023

GPT-4 and frontier multimodal LLMs

Key people: OpenAI and broader frontier labs

Why it mattersGPT-4 is presented as a large multimodal model accepting image and text inputs and producing text outputs, with strong performance on professional and academic benchmarks.

↑ back to top

2024

Real-time multimodal models

Key people: OpenAI GPT-4o and others

Why it mattersMultimodality becomes more native: text, vision, and audio move toward one interactive interface rather than separate models. OpenAI described GPT-4o as faster and cheaper than GPT-4 Turbo, with stronger vision and audio capabilities.

↑ back to top

2024

Reasoning models and inference-time compute

Key people: OpenAI o1; later many others

Why it mattersA new emphasis appears: models that spend more computation at inference time to reason through hard problems. OpenAI described o1 as designed to “spend more time thinking” before responding.

↑ back to top

2024

Nobel recognition

Key people: Hopfield, Hinton; Baker, Hassabis, Jumper

Why it mattersThe Nobel Prizes make the point that deep learning is no longer only an engineering technique: it has become a foundation for physics-inspired computation and scientific discovery.

↑ back to top

2025

Open reasoning models and RL-for-reasoning wave

Key people: DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o-series, others

Why it mattersDeepSeek-R1 made reinforcement learning for reasoning and open/distilled reasoning models a major public milestone. This pushed the frontier conversation toward reasoning, inference-time computation, and reproducible/open systems.

↑ back to top

2025–2026

Unified multimodal/agentic systems

Key people: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek, open-source ecosystem

Why it mattersThe frontier shifts from “a model” to “a system”: multimodal input/output, tool use, code execution, retrieval, memory, agents, and workflow automation. OpenAI’s GPT-5 and GPT-5.5 releases frame this as a move toward unified systems that can do useful work across modalities and tasks.
